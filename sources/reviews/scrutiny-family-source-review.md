# Source review: Public scrutiny and reputation family

**Events in the family:** Backlash to something the organization said or did · Rumor or misinformation about the organization · Pressure from activists, campaigners or investors
**Scope:** United States and European Union
**Prepared:** 26 September 2026
**Purpose:** source basis for the Public scrutiny family protocol

---

## 0. How these sources were read

Same method as the earlier reviews. Legal texts were opened on official or standard sites; the EU Market Abuse Regulation was read from the UK's retained copy, and the NYSE rule from the Exchange's 2015 rule-filing exhibit on sec.gov. The retrieval tool summarised some passages; direct quotes are marked as such. Nothing is cited from memory.

---

## 1. The short answer

None of the three events has its own protocol, so today they get only the core protocol, plus the Listed company overlay for listed organizations.

What these events share is that others are setting the story: the organization is answering a criticism, a claim or a demand it didn't write. The account problem is **stating fairly what is being said, correcting what is false without spreading it, not denying what is true, and giving reasons rather than only regret.** Five sources speak to it:

- **Correcting false claims (Lewandowsky et al., *The Debunking Handbook 2020*):** lead with the fact, "repeat the misinformation, only once, directly prior to the correction", explain why it is wrong, and restate the fact. Backfire effects "are not as common as we used to think".
- **Rumours about listed companies (EU Market Abuse Regulation, Art. 17(7); NYSE Listed Company Manual 202.03):** a sufficiently accurate rumour about inside information whose disclosure was delayed must be answered by disclosing it; under the NYSE rule, false rumours "should be promptly denied or clarified", and correct ones need "an immediate candid statement".
- **Attacks on critics in shareholder campaigns (SEC Rule 14a-9):** in proxy solicitations, material that "impugns character, integrity or personal reputation … without factual foundation" may be misleading.
- **Responding to online backlash (Herhausen et al. 2019):** across 472,995 negative posts on 89 large US companies' Facebook pages, more explanation, rather than more empathy, contained firestorms better.

## 2. The sources, by tier

### Tier 1 — Binding law and listing rules

**1.1 Regulation (EU) No 596/2014 (Market Abuse Regulation), Article 17(7).**
Link read: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2014/596/article/17 (UK retained copy; the EU text is the same apart from UK-specific terms)
*Read status:* opened and read (Article 17(7)).
*Key material:* where disclosure of inside information has been delayed and confidentiality "is no longer ensured", the issuer must disclose it "as soon as possible". This "includes situations where a rumour explicitly relates to inside information the disclosure of which has been delayed … where that rumour is sufficiently accurate to indicate that the confidentiality of that information is no longer ensured."
*Binds:* issuers with securities on EU markets. (Likely already registered from the CEO departure review, as part of the Listed company overlay's sources.)

**1.2 NYSE Listed Company Manual, Section 202.03, "Dealing with Rumors or Unusual Market Activity".**
Link read: https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nyse/2015/34-75809-ex5.pdf (2015 rule-filing exhibit)
*Read status:* opened, partly read (the section, as returned). The current Manual was not opened.
*Key material:* "If rumors are in fact false or inaccurate, they should be promptly denied or clarified." "If rumors are correct or there are developments, an immediate candid statement to the public as to the state of negotiations or of development of corporate plans in the rumored area must be made directly and openly." Such statements are "essential despite the business inconvenience".
*Binds:* NYSE-listed companies (a listing standard, not a statute).

**1.3 SEC Rule 14a-9, 17 CFR 240.14a-9, with its Note.**
Link read: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.14a-9
*Read status:* opened, partly read (paragraph (a) and Note example (b)).
*Key material:* no proxy solicitation may contain a statement "false or misleading with respect to any material fact". Note example (b): "Material which directly or indirectly impugns character, integrity or personal reputation, or directly or indirectly makes charges concerning improper, illegal or immoral conduct or associations, without factual foundation."
*Binds:* anyone soliciting proxies from shareholders of SEC-registered companies. Relevant to activist and investor campaigns only.

### Tier 2 — Expert consensus

**2.1 Lewandowsky, S., Cook, J., Ecker, U. K. H., et al. (22 authors) (2020). *The Debunking Handbook 2020*. doi:10.17910/b7.1182.**
Link read: https://skepticalscience.com/docs/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf (citation confirmed at https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/scholcom/245/)
*Read status:* opened, partly read (pages 9 and 12, as returned).
*Key material:* a debunking has four parts: FACT ("State the truth first"), MYTH ("Repeat the misinformation, only once, directly prior to the correction"), FALLACY ("Explain why misinformation is wrong"), FACT ("State the truth again"). "Needless repetitions of the misinformation should be avoided." "Backfire effects are not as common as we used to think … Do not refrain from attempting to debunk."
*Weight:* a consensus document by 22 misinformation researchers, summarising the research rather than reporting one study. Written mainly about public-interest misinformation (science, health), applied here to claims about an organization.

### Tier 3 — Research

**3.1 Herhausen, D., Ludwig, S., Grewal, D., Wulf, J., & Schoegel, M. (2019). "Detecting, Preventing, and Mitigating Online Firestorms in Brand Communities." *Journal of Marketing*, 83(3). doi:10.1177/0022242918822300.**
Link read: https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/159387363/Detecting_preventing_and_mitigating_online_firestorms_in_brand_communities.pdf
*Read status:* opened, partly read (summary of data and findings, as returned).
*Design:* 472,995 negative posts in the Facebook communities of 89 S&P 500 firms, October 2011 to January 2016.
*Findings:* "more explanation, rather than more empathy, in firm responses is better suited to contain negative eWOM with more intensive high-arousal emotions" (explanation reduced virality by up to 10%); early apologies and moving the conversation offline helped at first but backfired once a firestorm had grown; immediate compensation increased virality.
*Weight:* one large observational study of customer complaints on one platform. Supports giving reasons rather than only sympathy; says nothing about statements to employees or investors.

## 4. How good the evidence is

Mixed but usable. The rules on rumours are binding and specific, but only for listed companies. The debunking advice is an expert consensus built on a large body of research, though mostly about public-interest topics. The firestorm study is large but observational, about customer complaints on one platform. Nothing tests messages about activist campaigns except the proxy rule, which only says what not to do. The labels say so.

---

## 5. Known failure patterns

**Documented in a source**
1. The false claim repeated prominently, or several times, before the correction (Debunking Handbook: repetition increases perceived truth).
2. A true rumour denied or called speculation (the reason for MAR Art. 17(7) and NYSE 202.03).
3. Attacks on critics' character in shareholder campaigns (Rule 14a-9 Note (b)).
4. Sympathy without explanation in reply to angry criticism (Herhausen et al. 2019).

**Practitioner observation, not sourced (label as judgement)**
5. Conditional regret: "we regret if anyone was offended", "we're sorry you feel that way".
6. A claim called "false" or "misinformation" with no word on what is true.
7. "We have listened to the feedback" with nothing said about what changes.
8. Critics dismissed as "a vocal minority", "agenda-driven" or "misinformed".

## 6. Scope limits

- **Defamation** and the risks of naming critics: for counsel.
- **Platform rules** on takedowns and reporting false content: not reviewed.
- **EU Digital Services Act** (platforms' duties on illegal content): not reviewed; it governs platforms, not the organization's own statement.
- **Proxy-contest practice** beyond Rule 14a-9 (Schedule 14A filings, universal proxy rules): not reviewed.
- **Whether to respond at all:** no source gives a rule for when silence is better. Left to a reviewer question.

## 8. What nobody has established

1. When responding to a rumour spreads it further than ignoring it would.
2. Whether stating critics' concerns fairly earns trust from the wider audience, not just the critics.
3. What an organization owes activists or campaigners outside a shareholder vote.
