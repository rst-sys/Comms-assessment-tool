# Source review: Change readiness overlay

**Applies to:** messages to employees about a change they will have to make: a restructuring or reorganization, a major policy change, a new or updated strategy, a merger or acquisition (and, if added, a new system, process or way of working)
**Scope:** United States and European Union
**Prepared:** 27 September 2026
**Purpose:** source basis for a Change readiness overlay

---

## 0. How these sources were read

Same method as the earlier reviews. The central papers are behind publishers' paywalls, several of which blocked retrieval; where a paper was read through a copy or record other than the publisher's, it says so. Nothing is cited from memory. One source named in the earlier discussion (Kotter's 1995 article) could not be opened and is not relied on.

## 1. The short answer

The tool already covers *what* changes and *whether it's decided* (Workforce family; Workforce impact overlay) and *why* (the framework's causation dimension). What it doesn't check is whether a change message gives people what they need to be ready to make the change.

There is an unusually well-developed research base for that. Armenakis and colleagues, drawing on organizational research back to the 1940s, identify **five beliefs** that determine whether people buy into a change, and say change agents must "anticipate, consider, and plan to influence and shape these beliefs":

| Belief | Definition (Armenakis & Harris 2009, p. 129) |
|---|---|
| Discrepancy | "the belief that a change is needed; that there is a significant gap between the current state of the organization and what it should be" |
| Appropriateness | "the belief that a specific change designed to address a discrepancy is the correct one for the situation" |
| Efficacy | "the belief that the change recipient and the organization can successfully implement a change" |
| Principal support | "the belief that the formal leaders … are committed to the success of a change and that it is not going to be another passing fad or program of the month" |
| Valence | "the belief that the change is beneficial to the change recipient; there is something of benefit in it for them" |

The beliefs have a validated measurement scale (Armenakis et al. 2007, four studies), and independent studies support the pieces a message can carry: **information about the change, self-efficacy for coping and participation predicted openness to change** (Wanberg & Banas 2000), and **explanations improve fairness judgements and reduce retaliation and withdrawal, especially after unfavourable outcomes** (Shaw, Wild & Colquitt 2003, 54 samples).

Two of the five (discrepancy, appropriateness) are largely covered by the framework's causation dimension. The overlay should concentrate on the three nothing else checks: **efficacy, principal support and valence**, plus the choice of *this* change over alternatives.

## 2. The sources, by tier

### Tier 1 — Binding law (already registered)

**Directive 2002/14/EC, Art. 4(2)(c):** employees in EU undertakings must be informed and consulted on "decisions likely to lead to substantial changes in work organisation or in contractual relations". Already the basis of the Workforce family's consultation check. Not repeated in the overlay.

### Tier 5 — Research

**5.1 Armenakis, A. A., & Harris, S. G. (2009). "Reflections: our journey in organizational change research and practice." *Journal of Change Management*, 9(2), 127–142. doi:10.1080/14697010902879079.**
Link read: https://www.academia.edu/24231547/Reflections_our_Journey_in_Organizational_Change_Research_and_Practice (author-posted copy; the publisher's page returned 403)
*Read status:* opened, partly read (pages 129 and 135, as returned).
*Key material:* the five definitions above; "the task facing change agents is to anticipate, consider, and plan to influence and shape these beliefs in pursuit of readiness for change, implementation support, and change commitment" (p. 129); "change recipients must believe the five components" and "change agents must devote the time to this reinforcement" (p. 135).
*Weight:* a reflective overview by the framework's authors, summarising their research programme. The main citable statement of the five beliefs.

**5.2 Armenakis, A. A., Bernerth, J. B., Pitts, J. P., & Walker, H. J. (2007). "Organizational change recipients' beliefs scale: Development of an assessment instrument." *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science*, 43(4), 481–505. doi:10.1177/0021886307303654.**
Link: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0021886307303654
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract, as returned).
*Key material:* based on research "dating to the 1940s", five precursors "that determine the degree of buy-in" by change recipients; a 24-item scale developed and validated across four studies, usable "at any stage of the change process".
*Weight:* shows the five beliefs can be measured reliably. Doesn't test messages.

**5.3 Wanberg, C. R., & Banas, J. T. (2000). "Predictors and outcomes of openness to changes in a reorganizing workplace." *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 85(1), 132–142.**
Link read: https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/predictors-and-outcomes-of-openness-to-changes-in-a-reorganizing-/ (university record; PubMed and APA blocked retrieval)
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract as summarised by the tool; sample size not shown).
*Key material:* a longitudinal study in a reorganizing workplace; **information about the changes, change-related self-efficacy and participation in decision-making** predicted greater openness; lower openness went with lower job satisfaction, more irritation at work and stronger intentions to quit.
*Weight:* one longitudinal field study. The closest support for a message giving information and building confidence to cope.

**5.4 Shaw, J. C., Wild, E., & Colquitt, J. A. (2003). "To justify or excuse?: A meta-analytic review of the effects of explanations." *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 88(3), 444–458.**
Link read: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12814294/
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract, as returned).
*Key material:* 54 independent samples; explanations significantly improved justice judgements and reduced retaliation and withdrawal; effects were stronger after unfavourable outcomes; excuses were more effective than justifications.
*Weight:* a meta-analysis, the strongest evidence in this review. Supports explaining *why*, which the framework already requires; the excuses-over-justifications finding is noted but not built into a check.

**5.5 Oreg, S., Vakola, M., & Armenakis, A. (2011). "Change recipients' reactions to organizational change: A 60-year review of quantitative studies." *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science*, 47(4), 461–524.**
Link: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0021886310396550
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract as summarised).
*Key material:* 79 quantitative studies, 1948–2007; reactions to change depend on individual characteristics, context, the change process, perceived benefit or harm, and the change itself. The abstract as returned gave no specific findings on communication.
*Weight:* background; confirms perceived benefit or harm (valence) and process are recognised antecedents.

**5.6 Rafferty, A. E., Jimmieson, N. L., & Armenakis, A. A. (2013). "Change readiness: A multilevel review." *Journal of Management*, 39(1), 110–135.**
Read: abstract. Notes "substantial agreement about the key cognitions that underlie change readiness" and argues feelings matter too. Background only.

**5.7 Armenakis, A. A., Harris, S. G., & Mossholder, K. W. (1993). "Creating readiness for organizational change." *Human Relations*, 46(6), 681–703.**
Read: abstract. The founding paper on readiness as "beliefs, attitudes, and intentions". Background only.

### Tier 6 — Practitioner models (not relied on)

Kotter's eight-step model (1995, *Harvard Business Review*) and Prosci's ADKAR model are widely used. Kotter's article could not be opened; neither is cited as evidence. Where they agree with the research (for example on explaining the need for change and reinforcing it), the research is the basis.

## 4. How good the evidence is

Better than most families. The five beliefs rest on a long research programme and a validated scale; one longitudinal study and one meta-analysis support information, confidence and explanation. The gap is the usual one: **no study tests whether a message that addresses the five beliefs is trusted more**. The research measures what people believe, not what a message said. Applying it to a single message is professional judgement; the labels say research because each check maps to a named, measured belief.

---

## 5. Known failure patterns

**Documented in a source**
1. Change announced without addressing why it's needed or why this option (the reason the framework names discrepancy and appropriateness).
2. "Program of the month": people doubting leaders' commitment after earlier changes faded (named in the definition of principal support).
3. Information withheld during reorganization, lowering openness (Wanberg & Banas 2000).

**Practitioner observation, not sourced (label as judgement)**
4. Change presented only as opportunity ("exciting journey", "empowered"), with no word on what gets harder.
5. "Training and support will be provided", with no what, when or who.
6. Telling people how to feel ("embrace the change", "we're all excited").

---

## 6. Scope limits

- **Consultation and bargaining law:** covered by the Workforce family and Workforce impact overlay; not repeated.
- **Change sequencing** (a series of messages over months): the tool reviews one draft at a time. The overlay asks what *this* message owes readers, not whether a campaign is complete.
- **Feelings:** Rafferty et al. argue readiness is partly emotional. The overlay checks what a message says, not how it makes people feel.

## 8. What nobody has established

1. Whether a message that addresses all five beliefs produces more readiness than one that addresses some.
2. How much a single message can shift beliefs formed by experience of past changes.
3. How honest a message can be about losses before it lowers readiness rather than raising trust.

---

## Recommendation for the tool

Build a **Change readiness overlay** with four checks, three triggers and three reviewer questions, triggered when the audience includes employees and the event is a restructuring or reorganization, a major policy change, a new or updated strategy, or a merger or acquisition. Consider a new Workforce family event, "New system, process or way of working", which the current list lacks.
