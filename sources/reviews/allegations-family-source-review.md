# Source review: Allegations and misconduct family

**Events in the family:** Allegations against a leader · Harassment, discrimination or culture allegations · Fraud or financial misconduct · Investigation, lawsuit or regulatory action
**Scope:** United States and European Union
**Prepared:** 26 September 2026
**Purpose:** source basis for the Allegations family protocol

---

## 0. How these sources were read

Same method as the earlier reviews. Legal texts were opened on official or standard sites (EUR-Lex, eCFR, Cornell LII, justice.gov). The retrieval tool abbreviated or summarised some passages; those are marked "(abbreviated)". Two sources that would have been central turned out to be withdrawn in 2026 and are **not** used (section 7). Nothing is cited from memory.

---

## 1. The short answer

None of the four events has its own protocol, so today they get only the core protocol, plus the Listed company overlay for listed organizations and the People harmed overlay where people have been harmed.

What these events share is a claim of wrongdoing whose truth isn't yet settled. The account problem is: **what is alleged, what is established, who is finding out, and who is protected while they do.** Four kinds of source speak to it:

- **Presumption of innocence (EU Directive 2016/343, Art. 4):** public authorities must not refer to a suspect "as being guilty" before guilt is proved. Binds authorities in criminal cases only; the principle transfers to organizations by analogy.
- **Research on denial (Kim et al. 2004):** denial can repair trust for integrity violations, but apology works better "when there was subsequent evidence of guilt". A flat denial before the facts are in is a bet.
- **Investigations (US DOJ, *Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs*, Sept 2024):** prosecutors ask whether investigations were "properly scoped, and were independent, objective, appropriately conducted, and properly documented".
- **Protection for people who report (EU Whistleblower Directive 2019/1937; 18 U.S.C. § 1514A; SEC Rule 21F-17):** reporters' identities are confidential; harm to their reputation "including in social media" counts as retaliation; no one may impede reports to the SEC.

## 2. The sources, by tier

### Tier 1 — Binding law

**1.1 Directive (EU) 2019/1937 on the protection of persons who report breaches of Union law (Whistleblower Directive), Articles 16, 19 and 22.**
Link: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32019L1937
*Read status:* opened, partly read; quotes abbreviated by the tool.
*Key material:* Art. 16: the identity of the reporting person is to be kept confidential (abbreviated). Art. 19: retaliation is "any act or omission which occurs in a work-related context and causes detriment to the reporting person" and includes harm to "reputation, including in social media" (abbreviated). Art. 22(2): the identity of persons concerned by a report is also protected (abbreviated).
*Binds:* member states, through transposition; covers reports of breaches of EU law in listed areas (for example financial services, product safety, data protection), not every workplace grievance.

**1.2 Sarbanes-Oxley Act § 806, 18 U.S.C. § 1514A(a).**
Link: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1514A
*Read status:* opened, partly read (subsection (a), summarised by the tool).
*Key material:* companies with SEC-registered securities (and their subsidiaries, officers, contractors and agents) may not "discharge, demote, suspend, threaten, harass, or in any other manner discriminate against an employee" for reporting suspected fraud or securities violations, internally or to authorities.
*Binds:* US listed companies.

**1.3 SEC Rule 21F-17(a), 17 CFR 240.21F-17.**
Link: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-II/part-240/subject-group-ECFR3bd6e6ae1e9d09d/section-240.21F-17
*Read status:* opened and read (paragraph (a)).
*Key material:* "No person may take any action to impede an individual from communicating directly with the Commission staff about a possible securities law violation, including enforcing, or threatening to enforce, a confidentiality agreement."
*Binds:* everyone, as to communications with the SEC.

**1.4 Directive (EU) 2016/343 on the presumption of innocence, Articles 2 and 4(1).**
Link: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016L0343
*Read status:* opened, partly read.
*Key material:* Art. 4(1): "for as long as a suspect or an accused person has not been proved guilty according to law, public statements made by public authorities … do not refer to that person as being guilty." Art. 2: applies to natural persons in criminal proceedings.
*Binds:* public authorities, in criminal proceedings. **Not organizations.** Used here only as a recognised statement of the principle, applied by analogy.

### Tier 2 — Official guidance

**2.1 US Department of Justice, Criminal Division, *Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs* (updated September 2024), section on investigation of misconduct and on confidential reporting.**
Link: https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-fraud/page/file/937501/dl
*Read status:* opened, partly read (the two sections).
*Key material:* "How has the company ensured that the investigations have been properly scoped, and were independent, objective, appropriately conducted, and properly documented?" On retaliation: "Does the company have an anti-retaliation policy?" and whether employees "who reported internally [are] treated differently than others involved in misconduct who did not".
*Binds:* nothing; it is how federal prosecutors assess a company's compliance program. Written for investigations, not for messages about them.

### Tier 3 — Research

**3.1 Kim, P. H., Ferrin, D. L., Cooper, C. D., & Dirks, K. T. (2004). "Removing the shadow of suspicion." *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 89(1), 104–118.** (Already in the core protocol source review.)
*Read status:* abstract only (SSRN); full text blocked.
*Finding (abstract):* trust was repaired better when parties "apologized for violations concerning matters of competence but denied culpability for violations concerning matters of integrity," and when they "apologized for violations when there was subsequent evidence of guilt."
*Why it matters here:* an allegation of misconduct is an integrity question, where denial can work, but only if it holds. A denial issued before the facts are established risks the worst outcome if guilt later emerges.

### Already reviewed, relevant here

- **Core protocol:** estimates marked as estimates (WHO, CDC CERC). "Alleged vs established" is the sharper form for this family.
- **People harmed overlay:** harm acknowledged; support people can reach.
- **CEO departure protocol:** Wells Fargo board report (gap between a board's finding and its public statement). Applies when an allegation leads to an exit, filed as a departure.

## 4. How good the evidence is

The law is strong on protecting reporters but says nothing about what a public statement must contain. The presumption of innocence is binding only on authorities. The DOJ document is about investigations, not messages. The only research is one set of experiments, read in abstract. So: one check rests on law applied to communications; one on guidance applied by analogy; one on law applied by analogy with research support; one on judgement. The labels say so.

## 5. Known failure patterns

**Documented in a source**
1. Reporters identified or disparaged (the reason the Whistleblower Directive names reputational harm, including on social media, as retaliation).
2. Confidentiality used to discourage reports to a regulator (the reason for SEC Rule 21F-17).
3. Denials that later prove false (Kim et al. 2004: apology outperforms denial once evidence of guilt emerges).
4. Investigations that aren't independent or properly scoped (the reason prosecutors ask).

**Practitioner observation, not sourced (label as judgement)**
5. "We take these allegations very seriously" standing in for any statement of what is being done.
6. An investigation called "independent" or "thorough" with no word on who runs it or to whom they report.
7. A settlement, dismissal or closed inquiry presented as proof of innocence when it was not a finding on the facts.
8. The accused person praised, or the matter pre-judged, in the same statement that announces the review.

## 6. Scope limits

- **Defamation law** (US and EU member states) not reviewed. Whether a statement about a named person is defamatory is for counsel.
- **Data protection for allegations and criminal-offence data** (GDPR Art. 10) not reviewed; belongs to the Personal data overlay.
- **US employment law on harassment investigations** (the Faragher/Ellerth defence) not read; the EEOC's 2024 guidance that summarised it was rescinded (section 7).
- **Litigation holds, privilege and settlement confidentiality** not reviewed.
- **Sector regulators' rules** (for example financial-services conduct regimes) not reviewed.

## 8. What nobody has established

1. Whether naming who investigates, and how independently, makes readers trust the outcome more.
2. Whether saying "we don't yet know" about an allegation costs or earns trust compared with a denial, outside the laboratory.
3. How much an organization should say about interim steps (such as leave) without implying a finding.
