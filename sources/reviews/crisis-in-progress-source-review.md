# Source review: Crisis in progress overlay

**Applies to:** any message sent while a crisis is still unfolding ("Where do things stand?" = Still unfolding), whatever the event
**Scope:** United States and European Union
**Prepared:** 28 September 2026
**Purpose:** source basis for a Crisis in progress overlay (replacing the unbuilt Stage overlay)

---

## 0. How these sources were read

Same method as the earlier reviews. Coombs (2007) was read from an author-posted full-text copy; the main meta-analysis (Ma & Zhan 2016) still couldn't be opened, and its findings are taken from a peer-reviewed paper that summarises it, marked as such. Sources already read for earlier reviews are reused and not re-read. Nothing is cited from memory.

---

## 1. The short answer

The tool already checks crisis messages thoroughly, one message at a time: the core protocol rests on WHO and CDC crisis guidance and on Coombs, and most families are crisis families. Two things aren't checked:

1. **What has changed since the last statement.** Crises unfold through a series of messages. A statement accurate on day one can be wrong by day three. The communicators' codes require prompt correction of errors, and research on trust repair finds denials backfire once evidence of guilt emerges; nothing in the tool asks a follow-up statement to correct what went before.
2. **Whether the response fits the organization's responsibility.** The field's main theory, Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT), holds that "rebuild" responses (taking responsibility, making amends) "should be used for crises with strong attributions of crisis responsibility (preventable crises)", that playing the victim belongs only to crises where the organization really is one, and that mixing denial with apology "will erode the effectiveness of the overall response" (Coombs 2007, p. 172).

A caution from the evidence: the field's meta-analysis found that attributed responsibility is strongly linked to reputation, while response strategies have only "a positive, but weak" link (Ma & Zhan 2016, as summarised by Chon et al. 2022). What an organization does matters more than how it words its response. So the overlay checks that the response is **consistent with** the organization's responsibility, not that it uses a particular formula.

## 2. The sources

### Tier 4 — Professional codes (reused)

**IABC, *Code of Ethics for Professional Communicators*.** Read 22 September 2026 for the geopolitical review: "I communicate accurate information and promptly correct any errors." Binds IABC members only; no adoption date on the page. The PRSA Code (same review) also names failing to correct inaccuracies as improper.

### Tier 5 — Research

**5.1 Coombs, W. T. (2007). "Protecting organization reputations during a crisis: The development and application of Situational Crisis Communication Theory." *Corporate Reputation Review*, 10(3), 163–176.** (Already registered from the core review.)
Link read: https://www.academia.edu/6432881/Protecting_Organization_Reputations_During_a_Crisis_The_Development_and_Application_of_Situational_Crisis_Communication_Theory (author-posted copy)
*Read status:* opened, partly read (pp. 167 and 172, as returned).
*Key material:*
- Three crisis clusters (p. 167). **Victim:** "the organization is also a victim of the crisis" (natural disasters, rumours, workplace violence, product tampering). **Accidental:** "the organizational actions leading to the crisis were unintentional" (technical-error accidents and product harm). **Preventable:** "the organization knowingly placed people at risk, took inappropriate actions or violated a law/regulation" (human-error accidents and product harm, organizational misdeed).
- Guidelines (p. 172): "Rebuild crisis response strategies should be used for crises with strong attributions of crisis responsibility (preventable crises) regardless of crisis history or prior relationship reputation." "Victimage can be used as part of the response for workplace violence, product tampering, natural disasters and rumors." "The deny posture crisis response strategies should be used for rumor and challenge crises, when possible." "Maintain consistency in crisis response strategies. Mixing deny crisis response strategies with either the diminish or rebuild strategies will erode the effectiveness of the overall response."
*Weight:* the field's dominant theory. The guidelines are the author's recommendations, derived from theory and early experiments; they are not each separately tested.

**5.2 Ma, L., & Zhan, M. (2016). "Effects of attributed responsibility and response strategies on organizational reputation: A meta-analysis of situational crisis communication theory research." *Journal of Public Relations Research*, 28(2).**
*Read status:* **not opened** (publisher 403; Semantic Scholar returned no content; ResearchGate blocked). Findings as summarised in Chon, M.-G., Kim, J.-N., & Tam, L. (2022), *International Journal of Communication* 16, 2103–2125 (opened: https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/18185/3751): "attributed responsibility had a strong and negative association with organizational reputation"; "crisis response strategies had a positive, but weak, association with organizational reputation"; the authors conclude "publics expect organizations in crises to do more than simply use crisis response strategies".
*Weight:* the field's meta-analysis, known here only at second hand. Tempers any claim that wording alone repairs reputation.

**5.3 Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks (2004)** (already registered; abstract read). Trust was repaired better when parties "apologized for violations when there was subsequent evidence of guilt." Supports correcting an earlier denial once the facts turn.

### Tier 2 — Guidance (reused)

**WHO (2017), recommendation A.2** (already registered): communication "should include explicit information about uncertainties … and indicate what is known and not known at a given time." The words "at a given time" imply that what is known changes; the guideline doesn't say how to handle earlier statements.

## 4. How good the evidence is

The correction check rests on professional codes (binding only on members) and one set of experiments; no study tests correcting an earlier crisis statement. The responsibility check rests on the field's main theory, whose own meta-analysis finds wording matters less than responsibility itself. Both checks are sound as professional standards; neither is a measured effect of message content. The labels say so.

---

## 5. Known failure patterns

**Documented in a source**
1. Denial mixed with apology or compensation, which cancels out (Coombs 2007, guideline 8).
2. Playing the victim outside victim crises (Coombs 2007, guideline 2 limits victimage to victim crises).
3. A denial kept up after evidence of fault emerges (Kim et al. 2004).

**Practitioner observation, not sourced (label as judgement)**
4. An update that silently changes an earlier figure ("one batch" becomes "four batches") without saying the first statement was wrong.
5. "Out of an abundance of caution" used for a step the facts require.
6. The first statement's reassurance repeated after it has been overtaken.

---

## 6. Scope limits

- **Speed** (whether the organization spoke first or fast enough): can't be judged from a draft. The Arpan & Roskos-Ewoldsen "stealing thunder" finding (core review) stays unused.
- **Whether a correction creates legal exposure:** for counsel; a reviewer question asks.
- **Crisis type:** the overlay relies on the supplied context to show the organization's share of responsibility. Without it, the responsibility check can't fire, as with the Public scrutiny family's denial check.

## 8. What nobody has established

1. Whether correcting an earlier statement openly costs or earns trust, compared with quietly updating it.
2. Whether matching the response to responsibility, as SCCT recommends, measurably improves outcomes (the meta-analysis suggests the effect of any response is weak).
3. How often a crisis should be updated.

---

## Revision (0.2.0, 28 September 2026)

Counsel often limits what a crisis statement may admit. The responsibility check now accepts, where the context shows that constraint, a plain account of what the organization is doing to put things right without admitting fault; the meta-analysis above suggests action matters more than wording in any case. The denial-and-apology trigger no longer counts a declared legal constraint ("we can't comment on fault while legal matters are open") as denial.

## Recommendation for the tool

Build a **Crisis in progress overlay** with two checks, three triggers and three reviewer questions, switched on when "Where do things stand?" is "Still unfolding". Retire the Stage overlay stub. Add a prompt in the intake, shown when "Still unfolding" is chosen: paste any earlier statement into "Anything else we should know?".

Draft overlay: `protocols/overlays/crisis-in-progress.md`.
