# Source review: core protocol (checks every high-stakes message needs)

**Scope:** every named high-stakes event, any organization type. Sources are international or US/EU.
**Prepared:** 25 September 2026
**Purpose:** source basis for the Trust Assessment Assistant core protocol

---

## 0. How these sources were read

Every source was retrieved with a web-fetch tool that returns an extracted, model-rendered version of the page, not the page as a person would see it. "Opened and read" means the page was retrieved and its extracted text reviewed. Quotes are reproduced as the tool returned them. Where a quote carries weight, check it against the original before the tool relies on it.

Nothing below is cited from memory. Sources that were found but not opened are in §7, not §2. Sources already reviewed in the CEO departure and geopolitical source reviews (PRSA, IABC, Page Principles, ISO 22361, Coombs 2021) are cross-referenced, not re-read.

---

## 1. The short answer

**No law, standard or professional code specifies what every high-stakes organizational message must contain.** Binding law is event-specific and consequence-specific (see the CEO departure and geopolitical reviews), so it belongs in overlays and event protocols, not the core.

What does exist, and is reasonably strong, is **emergency and crisis risk-communication guidance**: a WHO guideline built from commissioned systematic reviews with graded recommendations, the US CDC's CERC framework, and an expert-panel set of best practices published in a peer-reviewed journal. These agree closely on four things a message should do: **say what is known and not known, tell people what they can do, acknowledge who is harmed, and be honest and timely.** Crisis-communication research adds a fifth: **before any reputation repair, give people the information they need to protect themselves and cope** (Coombs's "instructing" and "adjusting" information).

Two of the six current core checks have **no authoritative source**: *who decided* and *when the next update comes*. Experimental research supports the first indirectly (agentless language reduces the blame readers assign). The second rests on professional judgement. Both should be labelled as such.

The main caveat: the strongest guidance (WHO, CERC) was written for **public-health and emergency authorities**. Applying it to a company announcing layoffs or a CEO exit is reasoning by analogy, and the tool should say so.

## 2. The sources, by tier

### Tier 1 — Binding law and regulation

**None for the core.** Every binding obligation found in the earlier reviews is triggered by a specific event (a listed-company departure) or consequence (redundancies, danger to workers, material information). Those belong in event protocols and overlays. See `sources/ceo-departure-source-review.md` and `sources/geopolitical-event-source-review.md`.

### Tier 2 — Regulator and agency guidance (official, not binding)

**2.1 World Health Organization. *Communicating risk in public health emergencies: a WHO guideline for emergency risk communication (ERC) policy and practice.* Geneva: WHO; 2017 (catalogue date 10 January 2018). ISBN 978-92-4-155020-8.**
Links: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550208 · recommendations: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK540733/ · method: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK540719/
*Covers:* how authorities should communicate risk during health emergencies.
*Binds:* nobody. WHO guidance to member states and responders.
*Method:* a Guideline Development Group formed in 2015; twelve questions guided evidence reviews commissioned in 2016 and completed by January 2017; recommendations formulated with "evidence-to-decision tables" and anonymous voting. The guideline itself notes that "assessing the quality of the evidence was difficult" and that the panel accepted weaker evidence where grey literature "indicated that the recommended action would yield clear benefits."
*Scope:* "epidemics and pandemics, and humanitarian crises and natural disasters." Not organizational communication.
*Read status:* opened and read (recommendations page and method summary).
*Key material (each rated strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence):*
- A.1 Trust: communication should "be transparent, timely, easy-to-understand, acknowledge uncertainty, address and engage affected populations, link to self-efficacy, and be disseminated using multiple platforms."
- A.2 Uncertainty: communication "should include explicit information about uncertainties associated with risks, events and interventions, and indicate what is known and not known at a given time."
- C4.1: "Risk should not be explained in technical terms."
- C4.2: "Consistent messages should come from different information sources and emerge early on in the emergency."
- C4.3: "Messages should promote specific actions people can realistically take to protect their health."
*Why it matters here:* the only source in this review with graded recommendations built on systematic reviews. It directly supports the *confirmed vs not known*, *what readers should do* and *plain statement* checks.

**2.2 US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. *Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication (CERC) Manual*, Introduction chapter.**
Links: https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf · manual index: https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/php/cerc-manual/index.html · programme page: https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/php/about/index.html
*Covers:* crisis and emergency risk communication for "anyone who communicates on behalf of an organization."
*Binds:* nobody. Federal agency guidance and training material.
*Currency:* **conflicting dates.** The Introduction PDF is marked "2018 Update (CS 290397-A)". The manual index page says first published 22 November 2024 and last updated 20 November 2024, which looks like a site migration. The programme page was last reviewed 28 July 2025. Recording all three.
*Read status:* Introduction PDF opened and read (principles section); manual index and programme page opened and read.
*Key material, the six principles as returned:*
1. **Be First:** "Crises are time-sensitive. Communicating information quickly is crucial."
2. **Be Right:** "Accuracy establishes credibility. Information can include what is known, what is not known, and what is being done to fill in the gaps."
3. **Be Credible:** "Honesty and truthfulness should not be compromised during crises."
4. **Express Empathy:** "Crises create harm, and the suffering should be acknowledged in words."
5. **Promote Action:** "Giving people meaningful things to do calms anxiety, helps restore order, and promotes some sense of control."
6. **Show Respect:** "Respectful communication is particularly important when people feel vulnerable."
*Evidence claim:* the manual describes itself as "an evidence-based framework." The Introduction chapter does not itself cite the evidence for each principle in what was returned. Treat the principles as authoritative practitioner guidance, not as measured findings.
*Note:* the Introduction chapter, as returned, **does not say when or how a next update should be announced.** The other chapters (Messages and Audiences, Spokesperson) were not opened.

### Tier 3 — Standards bodies

**3.1 ISO 24495-1:2023, *Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines*.** Published June 2023, edition 1.
Link: https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html
*Covers:* principles and guidelines for plain-language documents, "with the widest application to documents intended for the general public."
*Binds:* nobody; a guidance standard.
*Read status:* opened, partly read (catalogue page and abstract only; full text paywalled).
*Why it matters here:* the standards anchor for stating the central fact in ordinary words. The four principles (relevant, findable, understandable, usable) are already cited in the Standards Library; this review did not re-verify their wording from the full text.

**3.2 ISO 22361:2022, *Security and resilience — Crisis management — Guidelines*.** Published 19 October 2022, edition 1; guidance.
Link: https://www.iso.org/standard/50267.html
*Read status:* catalogue page opened and read. **Clause 8 (crisis communication) not read; paywalled.** See the geopolitical review §3.1 for its table of contents, including 8.7 "Consistency of message."

### Tier 4 — Professional bodies and codes

Already reviewed; cross-referenced only.
- **PRSA Code of Ethics:** "Disclosure of Information", improper conduct includes "lying by omission" and failure to correct. Weak enforcement. (CEO review 2.15; geopolitical review 4.2.)
- **IABC Code of Ethics:** "I communicate accurate information and promptly correct any errors." (CEO review 2.16; geopolitical review 4.3.)
- **Page Principles:** "Tell the truth", "Prove it with action"; the 90/10 figure is rhetoric, not measurement. (CEO review 2.17; geopolitical review 4.1.)

None of them names the content a message must include.

### Tier 5 — Peer-reviewed research

**5.1 Seeger, M. W. (2006). "Best practices in crisis communication: An expert panel process." *Journal of Applied Communication Research*, 34(3), 232–244. doi:10.1080/00909880600769944.**
Link: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00909880600769944
*Read status:* **primary article not opened (paywalled).** The ten practices below are quoted from a secondary source: Veil, S. R., Anthony, K. E., Sellnow, T. L., Staricek, N., Young, L. E., & Cupp, P. (2020). "Revisiting the best practices in risk and crisis communication: A multi-case analysis." In *The Handbook of Applied Communication Research*, Vol. 1, pp. 377–395. Wiley. (Opened via https://www.academia.edu/71121481/.)
*Method, per the secondary source:* an expert panel convened by a US Department of Homeland Security Center of Excellence expanded Covello and Allen's (1988) "Seven Cardinal Rules of Risk Communication."
*The ten practices, as quoted by Veil et al.:* process approaches and policy development; pre-event planning; partnerships with the public; "Listen to others' concerns"; "Exhibit honesty, candor and openness"; "Collaborate and coordinate with credible sources"; "Meet the needs of the media and remain accessible"; "Communicate with compassion, concern and empathy"; "Accept uncertainty and ambiguity"; "Provide messages of self-efficacy by issuing specific information telling people what they can do."
*Weight:* expert consensus published in a peer-reviewed journal. It is not a measurement of effect.

**5.2 Coombs, W. T. (2007). "Protecting organization reputations during a crisis: The development and application of Situational Crisis Communication Theory." *Corporate Reputation Review*, 10(3), 163–176.**
Link read: https://scispace.com/pdf/protecting-organization-reputations-during-a-crisis-the-c56nbgq8xa.pdf (publisher page: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/palgrave.crr.1550049)
*Read status:* opened and read (relevant sections, via a full-text copy on scispace; not the publisher's copy).
*Key material:* instructing information tells stakeholders how to protect themselves; adjusting information helps them cope and includes what happened, corrective actions, and "an expression of concern for the victims." "The first priority in any crisis is to protect stakeholders from harm, not to protect the reputation." "To be ethical, crisis managers must begin their efforts by using communication to address the physical and psychological concerns of the victims."
*Weight:* the field's dominant theory, from a leading scholar. The base-response argument is ethical and theoretical. Coombs himself notes the field was "dominated by case studies" before experimental work.
*Why it matters here:* the organizational-crisis source for the *what readers should do*, *who is affected* and *what is being done* checks. It bridges the public-health guidance to organizations.

**5.3 Arpan, L. M., & Roskos-Ewoldsen, D. R. (2005). "Stealing thunder: Analysis of the effects of proactive disclosure of crisis information." *Public Relations Review*, 31(3), 425–433.**
Link: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0363811105000809
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract).
*Design:* experiment, n = 113, after pretests (n = 40).
*Finding:* organizations that disclosed the crisis first were rated significantly more credible (M = 1.59 vs 0.49; F(1,111) = 12.72, p < .001). Self-disclosure was also linked to the crisis being seen as less severe.
*Weight:* one student-scale experiment. Supports the value of timely, self-initiated disclosure. Relevant mainly to the Stage overlay ("not yet public"), not the core.

**5.4 Kim, P. H., Ferrin, D. L., Cooper, C. D., & Dirks, K. T. (2004). "Removing the shadow of suspicion: The effects of apology versus denial for repairing competence- versus integrity-based trust violations." *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 89(1), 104–118.**
Link read: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=398221
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract). The APA PsycNet page was blocked to automated fetching.
*Finding (abstract):* trust was repaired better when parties "apologized for violations concerning matters of competence but denied culpability for violations concerning matters of integrity," and when they "apologized for violations when there was subsequent evidence of guilt."
*Why it matters here:* not a core check. It warns that the right response depends on the kind of violation, which is an argument for the apology overlay and for allegation-family protocols, not for a universal rule.

**5.5 Fausey, C. M., & Boroditsky, L. (2010). "Subtle linguistic cues influence perceived blame and financial liability." *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 17(5), 644–650.**
Link: https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/PBR.17.5.644
*Read status:* opened and read (abstract and summary).
*Design:* three experiments; accidents described with agentive (transitive) or non-agentive (intransitive) verbs.
*Finding:* agentive descriptions led readers to assign more blame and higher financial liability, "even when people were able to see a video of the event."
*Replication:* reported replicated by Tonković et al. (2022), *Legal and Criminological Psychology*, doi:10.1111/lcrp.12219, according to Petersen & Almor (2025), *Frontiers in Psychology* 16:1498958 (opened and read). **The replication paper itself returned 403 and was not read.**
*Why it matters here:* the only empirical support found for the *who decided* check. It shows agentless wording measurably shifts responsibility away from the actor. It does not show that naming the decision-maker increases trust.

**5.6 Li, F. (2008). "Annual report readability, current earnings, and earnings persistence." *Journal of Accounting and Economics*, 45(2–3), 221–247. doi:10.1016/j.jacceco.2008.02.003.**
Link: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165410108000141
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract).
*Finding:* across more than 50,000 firm-years, "the annual reports of firms with lower earnings are harder to read (i.e., they have a higher Fog index and are longer)."
*Why it matters here:* documented evidence that organizations write less plainly when the news is bad. It supports the *central fact stated plainly* check as a response to a measured pattern. Limits: annual reports, not crisis messages; correlational; readability measured by Fog index and length.

### Tier 6 — Practitioner consensus

**Coombs, *Ongoing Crisis Communication*, 6th ed. (2021)** — see the geopolitical review 6.2. Table of contents only.

## 4. How good the evidence is

**The guidance is the strongest material, and it is borrowed.** WHO's recommendations rest on commissioned systematic reviews and are rated strong, but the underlying evidence is rated moderate at best, the panel admits assessment was difficult, and the scope is health emergencies. CERC calls itself evidence-based but, in the chapter read, states principles without citing evidence for each. Both were written for public authorities speaking to the public about physical risk. An employer announcing a restructuring is a different speaker with different incentives. The tool can cite them, but it should say it applies them by analogy.

**The research supports parts, not the whole.** Seeger 2006 is expert consensus, not measurement. Coombs 2007 is theory with an explicit ethical argument. The experiments (Arpan & Roskos-Ewoldsen; Kim et al.; Fausey & Boroditsky) are small or student-based and measure perceptions in the lab. Li 2008 is large-sample but about annual reports. **No study found measures whether a message containing these seven elements is trusted more than one that lacks them.** That is the same gap the CEO and geopolitical reviews found.

**The codes add character, not content.** PRSA and IABC support honesty, accuracy and correction. They don't specify what a message must contain.

## 5. Known failure patterns

**Documented in a source**
1. **Writing less plainly when the news is bad.** Harder-to-read, longer annual reports from lower-earning firms (Li 2008).
2. **Agentless description reduces the responsibility readers assign.** (Fausey & Boroditsky 2010; replication reported, not read.)
3. **Leading with reputation instead of the people affected.** Named by Coombs 2007 as the unethical order of priorities.
4. **Letting others break the news.** Lower credibility when the organization is not the first discloser (Arpan & Roskos-Ewoldsen 2005).
5. **Lying by omission.** Named as improper conduct in the PRSA Code.

**Practitioner observation, not sourced (label as judgement)**
6. Empathy language with no named group and no concrete effect ("our thoughts are with everyone affected").
7. Certainty the facts don't support ("fully contained", "no impact") while the situation is still being established.
8. A promised update with no time, channel or owner ("we will share more in due course").

---

## 6. Scope limits

- **Public-health origin.** WHO and CERC were written for authorities in emergencies. Their transfer to corporate, nonprofit and employer communication is by analogy.
- **Physical risk vs organizational decisions.** Most guidance assumes a threat people must protect themselves from. Many drafts the tool sees (a policy change, a price rise) involve no physical risk; *what readers should do* then often reduces to "nothing is needed from you yet", which the check allows.
- **Culture and language.** The research is mostly US and English-language. ISO 24495-1 gives examples in English only.
- **No law.** The core deliberately carries no legal checks; those arrive through overlays.

## 8. What nobody has established

1. **Whether a message with all seven elements is trusted more.** No study tests the combination, or any organizational message's content against later trust.
2. **Whether naming the decision-maker builds trust.** The research shows agentless wording lowers blame. It does not show that agentive wording raises trust, and it may raise perceived blame.
3. **Whether announcing the next update matters.** No source found requires or tests it.
4. **How emergency guidance transfers to non-emergency decisions.** No source addresses a restructuring, price change or leadership exit.
5. **How to weigh speed against accuracy.** CERC's "Be First" and "Be Right" pull against each other; no source gives a rule.

## Recommendation for the tool

Adopt the seven checks below as the core protocol, with each check's basis labelled as in §3. Keep "who decided" and "next update" as **declared judgement**, supported (in the first case) by research. Don't describe WHO or CERC as standards for organizational communication; describe them as emergency-communication guidance applied by analogy. Move the "be first" material (Arpan & Roskos-Ewoldsen; CERC "Be First") to the Stage overlay, since the core can't judge timing from a draft. Ma & Zhan (2016) and Seeger (2006) could not be obtained; on 25 September 2026 the owner decided to activate the protocol without them, with the limitation recorded in the protocol file.

The draft protocol is in `protocols/core-protocol.md`.

## Addendum (25 September 2026): overlap with the framework prompt

After this review was written, the framework prompt (`SYSTEM_PROMPT` in `src/engine/promptText.ts`) was compared with the seven checks in §3. Five of them are already in the framework, which every draft gets:

| Check in §3 | Where the framework already has it |
|---|---|
| Who decided | THE ACCOUNT ("who had authority over it"); AGENCY CALIBRATION; the agency and abstraction scan |
| Who is affected, and how | THE ACCOUNT ("who is affected and how") |
| What readers should do | THE ACCOUNT ("what the reader should do now, or that nothing is needed from them yet") |
| What is being done, and by whom | THE ACCOUNT ("what will change; who owns the change"); the 4.0 rule on specific actors and actions |
| When the next update comes | THE ACCOUNT ("when the next update comes, where it will appear, and a named way to ask a question") |

Repeating them in a protocol would count them twice. The core protocol (v0.2) therefore keeps only what the framework lacks:

- **The central fact first, in ordinary words.** The framework asks for the decision, but not that it come first or plainly.
- **Estimates marked as estimates at every stage.** The framework asks for known vs not yet known only when the situation is still unfolding.
- **Reputation before people** (trigger only), from Coombs 2007.

The sources in §2 still have a use for the five framework elements: they can be shown as those elements' "Grounded in" sources in the Standards Library.

| What will change, who owns it | CDC CERC "Be Right"; Coombs 2007 | guidance + research |
| When the next update comes, where, how to ask | WHO A.1 ("timely"); Seeger 2006 ("remain accessible") | judgement |
| Known vs not yet known (still unfolding) | WHO A.2; CDC CERC "Be Right"; Seeger 2006 | guidance |
