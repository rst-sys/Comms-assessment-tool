# Source review: Workforce and organization change family

**Events in the family:** Layoffs or job cuts · Restructuring or reorganization · Site, office or store closure · Major policy change (e.g. return to office, benefits) · Strike or labor dispute
**Scope:** United States and European Union
**Prepared:** 25 September 2026
**Purpose:** source basis for the Workforce family protocol

---

## 0. How these sources were read

Same method as the earlier reviews: pages retrieved with a web-fetch tool that returns extracted text, not the page as a person sees it. Quotes are as returned. Sources already opened in earlier reviews (Directive 2002/14/EC, Directive 98/59/EC, 20 CFR 639, the workforce-reduction protocol's sources) are cross-referenced, not re-read. Nothing is cited from memory.

One extraction error to note: the fetched summary of 29 U.S.C. § 158 paraphrased Section 8(a)(5) as "requires employers to refuse to bargain", which is backwards. The statute makes it an unfair labor practice for an employer to refuse to bargain. Only the verbatim quotes below are relied on.

---

## 1. The short answer

Layoffs, restructuring and closures are already well covered: the workforce-reduction protocol and the Workforce impact overlay carry their checks. **The gap is the other two events.** A major policy change (return to office, benefits) and a strike or labor dispute currently get only the core protocol.

What those five events share is a decision that changes employees' terms or working conditions. Three bodies of law speak directly to how such a change is communicated or made:

- **EU:** Directive (EU) 2019/1152 requires that a change to the terms an employer must state in writing, including **place of work** and **working time**, be given to the worker in a document "at the earliest opportunity and at the latest on the day on which it takes effect." That is the closest thing found to a rule for announcing a return-to-office change. Directive 2002/14/EC already requires information and consultation on decisions likely to change work organisation.
- **US, unionized workplaces:** the National Labor Relations Act bars an employer from changing wages, hours or working conditions before bargaining with the union to agreement or impasse (with narrow exceptions), and protects employer speech only if it "contains no threat of reprisal or force or promise of benefit."
- **US, disability:** EEOC guidance says modifying a workplace policy, including allowing work from home where the essential functions can be done there, can be a reasonable accommodation, and that an employee may ask "in plain English."

Research adds one relevant, well-established finding: across 183 studies, **informational justice** (explanations of why procedures were used or outcomes distributed) correlates with trust (r = .43). That supports the framework's existing demand for reasons; it doesn't add a new check.

## 2. The sources, by tier

### Tier 1 — Binding law

**1.1 Directive (EU) 2019/1152 on transparent and predictable working conditions in the European Union, 20 June 2019.** Transposition deadline 1 August 2022.
Link: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A32019L1152
*Read status:* opened, partly read (Article 6 and Article 4(2) points (b), (l), (m)).
*Key material:* Article 6: "Any change in the aspects of the employment relationship referred to in Article 4(2) … shall be provided in the form of a document by the employer to the worker at the earliest opportunity and at the latest on the day on which it takes effect." Article 4(2)(b): "the place of work; where there is no fixed or main place of work, the principle that the worker is employed at various places or is free to determine his or her place of work." Article 4(2)(l): "the length of the worker's standard working day or week."
*Binds:* member states, through transposition; national rules vary and were not reviewed.
*Why it matters here:* a change to place of work (return to office) or working time is a change to a term the employer must document, individually, by the day it takes effect. A group announcement is not that document, but a draft that announces such a change with no effective date, or after it has taken effect, is out of step with it.

**1.2 National Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. § 158 (Section 8).**
Link: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/158
*Read status:* opened, partly read (subsections (c) and (d), as quoted).
*Key material:* § 158(c): "The expressing of any views, argument, or opinion … shall not constitute or be evidence of an unfair labor practice … if such expression contains no threat of reprisal or force or promise of benefit." § 158(d): bargaining collectively is "the mutual obligation of the employer and the representative of the employees to meet at reasonable times and confer in good faith with respect to wages, hours, and other terms and conditions of employment."
*Binds:* most private-sector US employers; the bargaining duty applies where employees have a union representative.

**1.3 Directive 2002/14/EC (information and consultation)** — already reviewed (geopolitical review 1.1). Article 4(2)(c): information and consultation "on decisions likely to lead to substantial changes in work organisation or in contractual relations." Applies to policy changes, not only redundancies.

### Tier 2 — Regulator and agency guidance

**2.1 National Labor Relations Board, "Bargaining in good faith with employees' union representative (Section 8(d) & 8(a)(5))."**
Link: https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/rights-we-protect/the-law/bargaining-in-good-faith-with-employees-union-representative
*Read status:* opened and read. No date on the page.
*Key material:* an employer may not "make changes in wages, hours, working conditions, or other mandatory subjects of bargaining before negotiating with the union to agreement or overall impasse," except where the union prevents agreement, economic exigencies require prompt action, or the change is a discrete recurring event with notice and an opportunity to bargain.
*Caution:* the NLRB's standards for unilateral changes have been revised by successive Boards (a news item titled "Board Revises Standard on Employers' Duty to Bargain Before Changing Terms and Conditions of Work" appeared in search results and was not opened). Treat the specifics as liable to change.

**2.2 National Labor Relations Board, "Interfering with employee rights (Section 7 & 8(a)(1))."**
Link: https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/rights-we-protect/the-law/interfering-with-employee-rights-section-7-8a1
*Read status:* opened and read. No date on the page.
*Key material:* examples of unlawful employer conduct include: "Threaten employees with adverse consequences, such as closing the workplace, loss of benefits, or more onerous working conditions, if they support a union"; "Promise employees benefits if they reject the union"; "Coercively question employees about their own or coworkers' union activities or sympathies."

**2.3 US EEOC, *Enforcement Guidance on Reasonable Accommodation and Undue Hardship under the ADA*, 17 October 2002.**
Link: https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/enforcement-guidance-reasonable-accommodation-and-undue-hardship-under-ada
*Read status:* opened, partly read.
*Standing:* "The contents of this document do not have the force and effect of law and are not meant to bind the public in any way." It explains the ADA, which is binding.
*Key material:* "It is a reasonable accommodation to modify a workplace policy when necessitated by an individual's disability-related limitations, absent undue hardship." Work from home can be an accommodation where "the essential functions of the position can be performed at home." "To request accommodation, an individual may use 'plain English' and need not mention the ADA or use the phrase 'reasonable accommodation.'" Employer and employee "should engage in an informal process to clarify what the individual needs."
*Currency:* 2002; whether it has been revised was not checked.

### Tier 5 — Peer-reviewed research

**5.1 Colquitt, J. A., Conlon, D. E., Wesson, M. J., Porter, C. O. L. H., & Ng, K. Y. (2001). "Justice at the millennium: A meta-analytic review of 25 years of organizational justice research." *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 86(3), 425–445.**
Link read: https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/colquitt%202001.pdf (a faculty-hosted copy; PubMed returned a security challenge)
*Read status:* opened, partly read (abstract and informational-justice results as extracted).
*Finding:* 183 studies. Informational justice, defined as "explanations provided to people that convey information about why procedures were used in a certain way or why outcomes were distributed in a certain fashion," correlated with trust (r = .43) and with evaluation of the authority (r = .58).
*Why it matters here:* the strongest general evidence that explaining a workforce decision matters to trust. It grounds the framework's existing demand for reasons (causation), which the family doesn't need to repeat.

**Already in the registry and relevant:** Topa et al. (2022), psychological-contract breach, a systematic review of reviews whose authors call their conclusions "tentative"; Khaw et al. (2022), reactions to organizational change, pages 1–6 of 24 read. Both were read by the workforce-reduction protocol's author. Topa supports the question on reversing earlier commitments.

## 4. How good the evidence is

The law is specific but narrow. EU 2019/1152 governs individual written statements, not announcements. The NLRA bargaining duty applies only where there's a union. The ADA covers disability, not caregiving or commuting. Each check therefore applies these rules' logic to a group message, which is professional judgement where the law doesn't reach, and the labels should say so.

The research (Colquitt 2001) is strong on a general point, that explanations relate to trust, and says nothing about specific message content. No source found tests whether any particular content in a policy-change or strike message changes employee trust.

## 5. Known failure patterns

**Documented in a source**
1. Changing terms before bargaining with the union (NLRB 2.1).
2. Threatening closure or lost benefits, or promising benefits, in connection with union support (NLRB 2.2).
3. Treating an accommodation request as invalid because it didn't use the right words (EEOC 2.3, which says no magic words are needed).

**Practitioner observation, not sourced (label as judgement)**
4. A return-to-office policy announced with a start date but no word on who is exempt, or with leadership quietly exempt.
5. A benefits change described as "enhancements" or "simplification" when employees lose something (the core protocol's euphemism check covers this).
6. A reversal of an earlier promise (for example "remote work is here to stay") announced without acknowledging the earlier promise.

---

## 6. Scope limits

- **US state law** (for example state leave, scheduling and captive-audience laws) not reviewed.
- **EU national transposition** of 2019/1152 and 2002/14/EC not reviewed; works-council co-determination rights (for example Germany's) not reviewed, and they go well beyond the directives on working time and place of work.
- **Public-sector** labor relations not covered (the NLRA covers most private-sector employers).
- **Strikes:** the NLRA's protection of strikers and the rules on replacements were not reviewed; this family only checks for threats and promises and bargaining status. A Strike or labor dispute event protocol would need its own source review.
- **Research on return-to-office policies themselves** (whether they work) was not reviewed; it bears on the decision, not the message.

## 8. What nobody has established

1. Whether telling employees who is exempt from a policy increases or decreases trust.
2. How far ahead a policy change should be announced. EU law sets only a latest date (the day it takes effect) for the individual document.
3. What an employer may fairly say about a strike while it's under way beyond avoiding threats and promises.

---

## Recommendation for the tool

Adopt the four checks in §3 as the Workforce family protocol, with one trigger (threats or promises tied to union activity) and a question on reversing earlier commitments. Where the Workforce impact overlay also applies (layoffs, restructuring, site closures), its more specific elements replace the family's equivalents, so layoff drafts don't get the same check twice. Revisit "Strike or labor dispute" for its own event protocol once its source review is done.
